Condo Water Damage in Lynnwood, WA: Whose Insurance Pays?

A leak in a condo touches more than one policy and more than one household. Here is how the pieces are commonly divided and what to do in the first day.

Does condo insurance cover water damage in Lynnwood, WA? It can, but a condo loss usually involves two policies at once, the association's and your own, and the split between them depends on the building's governing documents.

That is why a stained ceiling from the unit above feels so confusing. This post explains the common structure in general terms, who tends to act first, and what to write down. Policies vary, so treat it as a map and not a ruling.

Two layers of coverage: the association and you

Condo buildings are commonly insured in layers. The association carries a master policy for the building and shared areas, and each owner carries a separate unit-owner policy, often called an HO-6 style policy. Where one layer ends and the other begins varies from building to building.

Some master policies cover the walls and fixtures as originally built. Others cover only the bare structure and leave everything inside the unit to the owner. The declaration, bylaws, and master policy summary spell this out, and the property manager can usually send you copies.

Your own policy often covers the interior finishes you installed, your belongings, and sometimes extra living costs if the unit cannot be used. Your insurer can tell you which of those your policy includes and what any deductible applies to.

A leak from the unit upstairs: who handles what

The most common condo call is a ceiling that starts dripping from the unit above. The first job is practical. Knock on the upstairs door, ask the neighbor to shut off the water, and call the property manager or the association's emergency line if no one answers.

Who ends up paying depends on the cause. A failed pipe inside a shared wall may be treated as a building item, while an overflowing washer or tub in the upstairs unit may fall to that owner's policy. The table below shows how that often looks, though your documents may differ.

Shared walls, ceilings, and the unit boundary

Water does not stop at a unit line. It runs along joists, drops through light fixtures, and wicks into the drywall of the unit next door. A small leak in one bathroom can wet three households.

That matters for cost and for coverage, because each affected unit may have its own claim and its own scope. The drying plan, however, works best when one crew checks all affected walls with moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras, so that no wet pocket is missed behind a neighbor's paint.

Ask the property manager whether the association already has a vendor on call. If it does, find out whether that vendor is covering common elements only or your unit as well.

What to document in a condo loss

Document as you would in a house, then add a few condo-specific items. Dates and names carry more weight when several parties are involved.

  • Photos and video of every wet surface in your unit, including the ceiling, walls, floor, and the light fixtures below the leak.
  • The upstairs unit number, the time you noticed the leak, and the name of whoever you spoke to there.
  • A copy of your policy declarations page and the association's insurance certificate or summary.
  • Moisture readings and a written scope from the restoration crew, so you can see what was wet and what was dried or removed.
  • Receipts for emergency purchases and any temporary housing costs.

Keep every message with the property manager in writing. If a call is the only option, send a short email afterward that records what was said.

Who to notify, in what order

Tell the property manager or the association first when the source may be a shared pipe, since the association may need to shut off a riser or send its own vendor. Then call the 24/7 line so drying starts in your unit. Report the loss to your own insurer the same day, because many policies expect prompt notice and prompt steps to limit the damage.

If you rent the unit instead of owning it, call the landlord right away and check your renter's policy for belongings. Where a flooded floor reaches an outlet or electrical panel, stay out and call the utility or 911 before anyone else.

How the drying and rebuild phases fit together

Drying comes first, and water mitigation is the service that removes standing water, sets air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, and tracks the readings until the materials are dry. The rebuild follows, and water damage repair covers new drywall, paint, flooring, and trim.

Condo rules can add steps. Many associations limit work hours, require elevator reservations, or ask for proof of insurance before a crew enters. Our crews also work in Everett and Bothell, where multi-unit buildings raise the same scheduling questions, so tell the dispatcher about building rules when you call.

Lynnwood note: half the households rent, so ask who lives above you

Census QuickFacts reports an owner-occupied housing rate of 51.1 percent for Lynnwood in 2020 to 2024, with 16,070 households. That means a large share of homes, in condo buildings and elsewhere, are rented. The person responsible for the leak upstairs may be an owner with a unit-owner policy or a tenant with a renter's policy and a landlord behind them.

The tie-back is to ask the property manager early who occupies the source unit and who their insurer is. The City's Development & Business Services department reviews building plans and issues permits, so if your rebuild involves more than paint and drywall, ask the Permit Center whether a permit applies and tell the association what the city says.

Questions we hear

Half of Lynnwood households rent, so what if the upstairs neighbor is a tenant?

The tenant, the owner of that unit, and the association may all be involved, and which one handles the loss depends on the cause and the documents. Ask the property manager for the owner's contact and for confirmation of who is arranging the fix.

Report the damage in writing, keep your own photos, and ask your insurer how it handles a loss caused by another unit.

Does the association's master policy cover my ceiling?

It may, if the ceiling is defined as part of the building and the cause is covered. Some master policies stop at the bare walls, and others include fixtures and finishes.

Request the master policy summary and the declaration from the property manager. Then ask your own insurer what your unit-owner policy picks up around it.

Do I have to let the association's vendor into my unit?

Many governing documents give the association a right of entry to stop damage to common elements. The details vary, so read yours.

You can still call your own crew to dry and document your unit. Tell both sides who is doing what so work is not duplicated.

What happens if I do nothing for a few days?

Wet drywall and insulation inside walls stay damp for a long time, and mold can start growing on damp materials in about 24 to 48 hours. Many policies also expect you to limit further damage.

Call the 24/7 line and your insurer promptly.
