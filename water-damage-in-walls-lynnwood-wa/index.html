Hidden Water Damage in Walls in Lynnwood, WA

Most wall leaks are found late, by a smell or a stain. Here is how crews find them early, open the wall and dry what is inside.

Water damage in walls in Lynnwood, WA often goes unseen for weeks. A pinhole in a supply line, a bad window flashing or a failed shower valve can wet a cavity long before paint shows a mark.

This post covers the warning signs, the tools that find hidden moisture, what opening a wall actually involves, and how plumbing, roof and siding leaks differ.

Signs that water is inside a wall

No single sign proves a hidden leak, but several together make it likely. Look for these before you cut anything.

  • A musty or earthy smell that is strongest near one wall.
  • Paint that bubbles, flakes or feels cool and damp to the hand.
  • Stains that grow, fade and return after dry weather.
  • Baseboards that swell or pull away from the wall.
  • Wallpaper seams that lift, or drywall seams that start to show.
  • A water bill that climbs with no change in how you use water.
  • The sound of running water when every fixture is off.

A rising water bill is worth taking seriously. If the meter keeps turning with every tap closed, water is leaking somewhere, and a plumber can narrow it down with a pressure test.

How crews find water behind painted drywall

Finding a wall leak without tearing the wall apart takes three tools. Each one answers a different question.

A moisture meter reads the water content of the board and the wood behind it. A pin meter pushes two small probes into the surface. A pinless meter scans the surface with a sensor and leaves no mark.

A thermal imaging camera shows surface temperature. Evaporating water cools a surface, so a damp patch often appears as a cooler shape. The camera does not see water, only temperature, so it points to where the meter should go next.

A small inspection hole lets a probe camera look into the cavity, which settles most doubts about what the readings suggest. The hole is about the size of a coin and is easy to patch, which makes it the lowest-cost way to confirm what the other tools suggest.

Plumbing, roof or siding: telling the source apart

The source decides who fixes it, so find it before any wall comes down. The table shows how the patterns usually differ.

A leak that follows the weather points to the roof or siding. A leak that never stops points to plumbing. Keep a note of when the stain changes.

What opening a wall actually involves

People expect the worst, but a careful crew opens only what the readings justify. Here is the usual sequence.

1

Confirm with readings

Meters and the camera map the wet area. The crew marks the edges on the wall so the cut follows the damage, not a guess.

2

Protect the room

Plastic sheeting and a taped-off work area keep dust out of the rest of the house. For dirty water, containment and HEPA air scrubbers are added.

3

Cut and remove

A clean cut through board along a stud line exposes the cavity. Wet board and wet insulation are bagged. Wiring and plumbing in the wall are checked before anything else is touched.

4

Inspect the cavity

The crew looks at the studs, bottom plate and back of the exterior sheathing for staining, softness or growth, and photographs them for your records.

Drying a wall cavity

An open cavity dries fast. Air movers blow across the studs, LGR dehumidifiers pull moisture from the room, and readings tell the crew when the wood reaches its dry standard. Most open walls dry in a few days.

Closed-wall drying needs more patience and more monitoring. The crew checks readings daily, and if the numbers stall, the next step is to open the wall. A wall that looks dry from outside can still hold wet framing, so the final decision rests on the meter and not on how the paint looks.

When the board is sound and only a small area is wet, some crews dry a closed wall by drilling small holes and pushing dry air into the cavity. This avoids removing board, but it works only if the water was clean and the insulation can dry. Dense-packed or blown-in insulation often holds water and may need to come out.

Drying is only half the job. The leak must be fixed or the cavity will be wet again. See how the water mitigation process sequences stopping the source and drying together.

Why walls stay wet longer than floors

A wall cavity is a closed box. Air barely moves inside it, so water that gets in has few ways to leave. Paint on the outside and house wrap on the other side slow evaporation further.

That is why a small leak can stay hidden for weeks. The board may feel dry to the touch while the bottom plate, where water collects, stays wet. Water also travels. It runs down inside the cavity and can show up as a ceiling stain on the floor below, far from the true source.

The same behavior holds in a home in Mountlake Terrace or Mukilteo, so the first step is always a moisture map rather than a guess about where the water entered. Mark the stain, then follow readings up and sideways from it until they return to normal.

Stop the source first

A dry wall that keeps getting wet is a failed repair. Plumbers test supply lines under pressure and camera-inspect drains. A roofer finds flashing and shingle failures. A siding contractor checks window flashing and caulk lines.

If the source is outside, ask for photos of the failed part so you can see the fix. Ask the pro to confirm the repair held after the next rain.

Mold needs moisture, so when moisture is gone and growth is present, testing and mold remediation follow the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard. Health concerns belong to a physician, not to a mold test.

Where DIY stops

A homeowner can check a bill, listen for running water and photograph a stain. That is where the useful DIY work ends.

Call a pro for any wall with wet outlets or switches, any wall that smells like sewage, large areas, exterior walls and anything that might involve the structure. Cutting open a wall without a plan spreads dust and may expose wiring.

If you must act before help arrives, shut off the main water valve, keep people away from the wall and leave electrical devices on that wall unplugged and unpowered. For anything beyond that, call the 24/7 line.

Lynnwood note: wires and gas lines share the wall

The City of Lynnwood's Public Works page lists Snohomish County PUD as the electric provider and Puget Sound Energy as the natural gas provider. Water in a wall often sits next to wiring, and exterior and kitchen walls can carry gas piping as well.

If water has reached an outlet, a switch or the panel, stay away from the wall. Call 911 for any electrical or gas risk, then call your electric or gas utility, and only after that call the restoration line. Restoration crews wait for the electrical hazard to be cleared before opening a wall near it.

Questions we hear

Who do I call if water in a Lynnwood wall may have reached outlets?

Stay away from the wall and call 911 if you see sparks, smell burning or smell gas. The City of Lynnwood's Public Works page lists Snohomish County PUD for electric service and Puget Sound Energy for natural gas, so those are the utilities to contact.

Once the hazard is handled, call the restoration line to start drying.

Can a thermal camera see water inside a wall?

It sees temperature differences on the wall surface, which often follow evaporating water. It does not see water itself, so a crew confirms every suspect area with a moisture meter.

How long can water sit in a wall before it causes problems?

Wet drywall and framing start to degrade within days, and mold growth can begin when materials stay damp. The longer water sits, the more material needs replacing.

Do I have to tear out the whole wall?

No. A careful crew opens only the wet section. Small inspection holes and readings show how far the water went.
